# RDM reflective assignment: competence rubric and cohort calibration

Version 1.0 · 6 October 2026 · Undergraduate Level 5 · August 2026 cohort

## 1. Scope, authority and source materials

This protocol is for the 1,500-word final reflective assignment in `RDM/Aug_2026/Reflective`. It defines the rubric before marking begins. No student marks are assigned by this document.

The assessment concerns the quality of reasoning, decision making, problem solving and reflection. Personal experience is the material through which these skills are demonstrated. Do not reward a dramatic life event, a fortunate outcome, agreement with the student's values, or extensive theory description in place of demonstrated competence.

Sources used to construct this protocol:

2. `L5 Assignment Grading Criteria (2).pdf`: University of Essex Online, Undergraduate Grading Criteria NQF Level 5, Version 1, August 2021. Knowledge and application appear on pp. 1–2; critical evaluation on p. 2; reading and presentation on pp. 3–4.
3. `RDM Reflective Assessment Piper Instructions.docx`: assignment-specific guidance and its four subcriteria under each of five main criteria.
4. `RDM - seminar 4 (1).pdf`: Expected Utility Theory (EUT), probabilities, subjective utilities, net expected utility, biases and Bayesian inference.
5. `RDM - Seminar 5 v2 (1).pdf`: reflective assignment, knowledge/application/evaluation distinctions, concrete implementation, problem-solving methods, source use and the five criterion weights.
6. `01-CLQ_grading_method.md`: useful general distinctions between mentioning, understanding, applying and evaluating. Its assessment-specific weights, Grice/psychographic requirements and caps do not apply here.
7. [Previous RDM competence-to-grade method, version 2.0](../../RDM_Aug_26_Case_Study/GRADING_METHOD_Independent_Subcriteria.md): independent competence assessment, cross-cohort calibration, criterion normalisation, university weighting, a common conversion and transparent rounding.

The university document supplies the qualitative standards and weights. **The target mean of 68 and the EUT/Bayes eligibility rule are tutor-specified moderation rules, not requirements stated in the university rubric.** Seminar 4 encourages EUT and/or Bayes; the additional restriction on 70+ comes from the tutor's instruction for this marking exercise.

This protocol supersedes the attached Piper document's direct 0–100 subcriterion marking procedure and its prohibition on additional overall caps, solely to implement the tutor's current instructions. The underlying 20 subcriteria are retained. It also extends the previous conversion method to handle bounds and the explicit eligibility cap. Do not import the Case Study's persuasion/argumentation source requirements, the CLQ's weights, or a previous cohort's conversion parameters.

## 2. Assignment requirements and criterion weights

Assess two distinct scenarios: one important past decision and one significant problem solved or attempted. A problem may involve choices, but two stages of the same event do not fulfil the requirement for separate scenarios.

For **each** scenario the student must provide:

- A concrete account of what actually happened and how they thought and acted at the time, separated from later module-informed interpretation.
- A module-informed replay showing what they would actually do differently: actions, questions, representations, calculations, tests or decision rules, supported by relevant sources.
- An explicit comparison and a reasoned judgement about whether the revised process might improve the decision or solution, including limitations and uncertainty.

Approximately 750 words per scenario is guidance, not a separate numerical requirement. A focused selection of methods applied well is preferable to a catalogue of theories. The student need not apply every technique, both EUT and Bayes, or a formal Toulmin model. Assess claims, reasons, assumptions and conclusions where relevant to the reflective argument.

## 3. Evidence scoring: only 0, 0.5 or 1

Score every subcriterion independently from the submitted work before calculating percentage grades or looking at the desired distribution.

- **1 — fully demonstrated:** accurate, sufficiently developed and concretely evidenced fulfilment of the subcriterion at the expected Level 5 standard. All its central requirements are demonstrated. This does not mean flawless or automatically 100%.
- **0.5 — partially demonstrated:** relevant evidence exists, but it is incomplete, uneven, underdeveloped, weakly connected to the scenario, or materially imprecise.
- **0 — not demonstrated:** absent, irrelevant, merely named where understanding/application is required, or so fundamentally mistaken that the required competence is not evidenced.

Where a row concerns both scenarios, full credit requires adequate evidence across both; strong treatment of only one normally earns 0.5. Judge the demonstrated extent rather than automatically assigning 0.5 for any fragment. A single paragraph can evidence several different competences, but record what it demonstrates for each separately.

Keep an evidence locator and a short reason for every score, especially borderline decisions. These are structured professional judgements, not judgement-free objective measurements. Do not infer intended work or treat the presence of a citation as proof of understanding. Do not score presentation fluency as evidence of critical analysis.

### C1. Knowledge and Understanding — 25%

| **C1C. Critical-thinking and reasoning knowledge** | Accurately explains relevant cross-cutting principles, such as Halpern's model, metacognition, bias, reasoning, causal inference or evidence evaluation, and connects them to the reflection. | Some correct explanation, but mainly generic, uneven or partly confused; conceptual links are weak. | No demonstrated understanding, or serious misconceptions replace the relevant principles. |

### C3. Critical Analysis and Evaluation — 25%

| Code and subcriterion | 1 — fully demonstrated | 0.5 — partially demonstrated | 0 — not demonstrated |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C3A. Comparison of naïve and informed approaches** | Analytically compares both pairs of approaches, identifying consequential changes in evidence, representation, reasoning, option generation, bias control, monitoring or action and explaining why those changes matter. | Comparisons are partly analytical but uneven across scenarios, or mainly describe differences without explaining their significance. | No meaningful comparison; the two accounts are merely placed next to one another or described as better/worse without reasons. |
| **C3B. Evaluation of whether the informed approach is better** | Reaches a supported, qualified judgement for both scenarios. Distinguishes process improvement from guaranteed outcome improvement and addresses hindsight, luck or uncertainty where relevant. | Gives plausible reasons for improvement but is overconfident, uneven, or insufficiently distinguishes process from outcome. | Assumes theory guarantees a better outcome, judges solely by success/failure, or gives no reasoned judgement. |
| **C3C. Methods, assumptions, evidence and alternatives** | Examines consequential assumptions, input/evidence quality, limitations and a credible alternative interpretation or approach. Explains how these affect confidence in the proposed improvements. | Identifies some limitations or alternatives but mostly lists them; evidence quality or implications receive limited examination. | Accepts methods, estimates or claims uncritically; citations, bias labels or generic pros and cons replace evaluation. |
| **C3D. Reflective insight, originality and argument quality** | Develops a coherent, independently reasoned insight about the student's thinking, supported by the scenarios and appropriate evidence. Makes justified connections or adaptations and recognises what retrospective personal evidence cannot establish. | Some relevant reflection and supported conclusions, but insight is conventional, thin or uneven; personal experience is occasionally overgeneralised. | Literature summary or unsupported self-description replaces reflection; conclusions do not follow from the reasons/evidence. |

**Critical-analysis lens:** assess how the student judges knowledge and inference, not simply whether they mention advantages and disadvantages. Look for evaluation of probabilities and utilities, diagnostic evidence, assumptions, competing explanations, causal claims, bias attribution, limits of transfer and the certainty warranted by a counterfactual replay. An honest conclusion that the module might improve the process without changing the eventual choice can be strong reflection.

Use the module reading list's designated edition and bibliographic details. Check actual use of **Halpern and Dunn, Thought and Knowledge**, rather than counting a different Halpern book or article as the required core text. The recommended Chapter 1 model and Units 8–9 guide relevant reading; they are not a requirement to recite every model component.

Do not impose a fixed source count in a 1,500-word assignment. Wider reading should be proportionate and relevant. Original theoretical works can establish a normative framework; empirical claims that a technique improves performance require appropriate research evidence. Do not demand an experiment to prove Bayes' theorem or treat a normative framework as an unconditional description of human behaviour. Primary research on argumentation and persuasion is not a separate mandatory requirement for this reflective assignment unless those methods are used and require support.

Judge the quality of source engagement under C4 and the quality of the student's inferential judgement under C3. These are distinct dimensions, not two automatic deductions for one missing citation. Do not deduct solely for using an older APA edition: the supplied university rubric explicitly permits this, while allowing advice on current conventions. Attribution problems warrant evidence-based academic-integrity review, not an unsupported allegation or an invented misconduct penalty.

C5A retains the attached instruction's architecture and balance function. Missing a scenario affects the relevant knowledge, application, comparison and structural evidence; do not add an unlisted overall cap. Do not penalise anonymisation, benign spelling variants or a student's life choices.

## 4. Eligibility for an overall academic grade of 70 or above

Record `EUT_applied`, `Bayes_applied` and `eligible_70_plus`, with evidence locations and reasons. Each method flag is `pass`, `fail` or `review`. Resolve all `review` flags before conversion. Eligibility is true if **at least one** method passes. Applying both earns no automatic bonus and does not guarantee a 70+ grade.

A qualifying application must occur in a module-informed replay of the student's own scenario. The decision scenario is the natural location, as encouraged in Seminar 4; a genuine application within the separate problem scenario also satisfies the tutor's at-least-one requirement. An unrelated illustrative exercise does not.

### EUT: what qualifies

The submission must show a meaningful comparison of at least two feasible actions, their relevant possible outcomes, probabilities, subjective utilities and a **worked probability-weighted net comparison** that informs the proposed choice:

`EU(action) = sum_over_outcomes[P(outcome | action) × U(outcome)]`

Look for outcomes represented sufficiently to make the comparison meaningful, coherent probabilities within each action, comparable utility values and an explanation of the result. A small table or calculation is sufficient; a large decision tree is unnecessary. Clearly identified illustrative estimates are acceptable in a retrospective replay. Do not require nonexistent historical probability data, but do not treat invented estimates as established facts.

An unweighted pros-and-cons list, utilities without probabilities, a lone favourable outcome, or saying “I would calculate expected utility” does **not** qualify. A transparent algebraic or natural-frequency calculation can qualify. An isolated arithmetic slip need not invalidate a sound demonstration; record it in the relevant score and review whether it changes the reasoning or choice. Fundamental confusion between probability and utility, or an incomplete net comparison, does not pass.

### Bayes: what qualifies

The submission must show:

1. At least two plausible competing hypotheses, including a hypothesis and its meaningful alternative where appropriate.
2. Their prior plausibility, informed by available background information rather than invented retrospective certainty.
3. Scenario-specific evidence and how likely it would be under the competing hypotheses: **diagnosticity**, not simply the existence of new information.
4. An explicit revision of relative confidence that takes account of both priors and likelihoods, with a consequence for the replayed judgement or action.

A concrete qualitative update can qualify, consistent with the attached instructions and Seminar 5. Merely saying “I would reconsider after collecting evidence” cannot. Numerical work must distinguish `P(E | H)` from `P(H | E)` and normalise where a posterior probability is reported. For two exhaustive hypotheses:

`P(H | E) = P(E | H)P(H) / [P(E | H)P(H) + P(E | not H)P(not H)]`

Do not demand false numerical precision. Evidence concerning the plausibility of rival explanations can be more useful than arbitrary numbers. Stronger work evaluates the reliability of the evidence, uncertainty in the prior and what further evidence would change the judgement; those features are assessed under C3 as well as the relevant application row.

### Applying the eligibility rule

- If either method passes: no EUT/Bayes cap; the overall academic ceiling is 100.
- If neither passes: the **overall academic mark is capped at 69**, including after whole-number rounding.
- Naming or citing a method alone does not remove the cap. A bibliography entry is not application.
- Keep the five criterion scores as assessed; do not cap every criterion at 69. A student may demonstrate strong presentation or problem-solving knowledge despite failing the gate.
- Failure of the gate is not an automatic zero in C1 or C2. Other methods can demonstrate those competences. Score what is present, then show the explicit overall cap separately.
- Source attribution is scored under C4; an isolated missing citation does not by itself erase an otherwise clearly demonstrated procedure.

## 5. First pass and cross-cohort calibration

1. Inventory every submission within the `Participant_*_assignsubmission_file` folders. Preserve exact participant IDs. Identify duplicate versions; resolve which is the submitted work before scoring. Rubrics, readmes, manifests and analysis outputs are not student submissions.
2. Read each complete submission, including tables, calculations and references. Check rendered pages where text extraction misses equations or images. Do not score inaccessible content as absent.
3. Independently score all 20 rows and the two method flags. Record concise evidence, the original/replay distinction, scenario separation and any word-count issue.
4. Compare work awarded 0, 0.5 and 1 on **each same subcriterion** across the entire cohort. Check borderline examples and every eligibility pass/fail against the same standards. Calibrate without seeing processed percentage targets where practicable.
5. Review especially: naming versus applying; describing versus evaluating; source citation versus evidence assessment; a numerical-looking table versus valid EUT; generic evidence updating versus Bayes; a planning list versus an implemented problem-solving method; hindsight versus justified counterfactual reasoning; personal illustration versus general proof; core-text substitution; and qualitative versus numerical Bayesian applications.
6. Revise scores only on evidence. Log participant, subcriterion or flag, old value, new value and reason. Freeze the calibrated matrix before deriving the percentage conversion.

The repository inventory on 6 October 2026 contains **17 participant folders**, each with one submission file. Recheck membership at marking time. Do not silently exclude low-scoring or capped students to obtain the preferred mean. Record unreadable files and unresolved cases as pending, not zero; finalise conversion only after the eligible cohort is complete. Never change raw scores simply to improve the grade distribution.

## 6. Raw totals, weighting and required distributions

For student `i`, criterion `c`, and its four subcriteria `j`, let `x_icj` be 0, 0.5 or 1:

`T_ic = sum_j(x_icj)` — raw criterion total, maximum 4.

`q_ic = T_ic / 4` — criterion mean on the common 0–1 scale.

`R_i = 0.25 q_i1 + 0.25 q_i2 + 0.25 q_i3 + 0.15 q_i4 + 0.10 q_i5`

The weights affect contributions, not the evidence codes or criterion averages. Do not add all 20 codes and interpret that sum as the correctly weighted overall. Do not mark the criterion percentages independently and then add a hidden uplift to the overall.

Before conversion, produce:

- **Student matrix:** all 20 codes; five raw totals and means; `R_i`; evidence and method flags.
- **Subcriterion distributions:** N, counts of 0/0.5/1, mean and population SD.
- **Criterion distributions:** N, raw-total frequency distribution, minimum, maximum, raw mean and population SD, and mean/SD of `q_ic`.

Use population SD throughout: `sqrt(sum((value − mean)^2) / N)`. A raw competence proportion is not yet an awarded percentage grade.

## 7. Percentage conversion for this assignment

### Configuration and interpretation

| Parameter | Current setting |
|---|---|
| Target academic cohort mean `M` | **68** |
| Conversion mode | **Mean-only**, with a common positive slope `b = 100` |
| Target SD | **Not specified**; report the observed SD |
| Per-criterion bounds | 0–100 |
| Overall academic ceiling | 100 if eligible; 69 otherwise |
| Stage to which the target applies | Academic overall **after the eligibility cap, before the word-count penalty** |
| Reported marks | Whole numbers; store full precision |

The tutor specified a high-60s mean, with 68 as the reference. Following the previous method's mean-only option, **do not silently impose the previous cohort's SD of 10**. This is cohort-relative moderation, not a forced normal distribution. An affine conversion preserves distribution shape where no bounds or caps apply; bounds and eligibility caps can change shape, spread and ordering. Do not claim the resulting distribution is Gaussian.

The offset is derived from the weighted overall distribution once and applied to every criterion. Do not centre each criterion separately on 68 or force every criterion to have the same variability. Differences such as stronger application than conceptual understanding remain visible, subject to the explicitly recorded effects of bounds.

### Common conversion, bounds and eligibility

First calculate the ordinary mean-only candidate:

`a_initial = M − 100 × mean(R)`

Define the bounded common conversion and the two distinct overall values:

`P_ic(a) = min(100, max(0, 100 × q_ic + a))`

`U_i(a) = sum_c(w_c × P_ic(a))` — weighted overall before the eligibility cap.

`cap_i = 100 if eligible_70_plus else 69`

`G_i(a) = min(U_i(a), cap_i)` — academic overall after eligibility.

If the candidate already gives `mean(G) = 68`, retain it. Otherwise solve **one common offset `a`** such that:

`mean_i(G_i(a)) = 68`

Use bisection on `a` in `[-100, 100]`, stopping when the mean error is below `1e-9` grade points. The mean is continuous and non-decreasing. Under the present caps its endpoints are 0 and at least 69, so 68 is mathematically attainable. If new caps or rules are introduced, verify that the target lies between the attainable endpoints before solving. A flat interval at the target can use the first solution reached by the documented bisection procedure.

This bounded, capped extension is intentional: simply adding an uplift after applying the cap could return ineligible students to 70+, while silently clipping an ordinary affine conversion would change the target mean. Record the initial and final offsets, all bound activations and each cap deduction.

Where no criterion hits a bound, `U_i = 100 × R_i + a` and criterion differences retain their original percentage-point spread. Where bounds apply, calculate `U_i` directly from the bounded criterion values; do not use that affine shortcut. The exact weighted reconciliation is always between the stored criterion values and **U**, not necessarily the capped mark **G**:

`G_i = U_i − eligibility_cap_deduction_i`

Criterion marks are calculated before multiplying them by their university weights. Weight changes require a complete recalculation of the common offset. Subcriterion evidence codes remain 0/0.5/1; do not relabel converted code values as independent subcriterion percentage grades.

### Interpretation and moderation safeguards

Norming can raise or lower marks without changing demonstrated competence. A three-level rubric also creates ties and cannot recover distinctions that were never recorded. A positive offset can give a non-zero converted value to a criterion with no demonstrated competence. Keep those raw codes visible in the internal sheet and flag such cases; never describe the uplift as new evidence of learning.

Check processed grades against the Level 5 descriptors: 60–69 denotes solid, competent work; 70–79 requires detailed understanding, highly competent application and effective evaluation; 80+ requires the exceptional features described in the university rubric. A mechanically high percentage or eligibility pass does not itself establish these qualities. The source rubric's overlapping printed fail ranges should not be used to create contradictory category labels; this workflow reports numeric marks rather than deriving new band boundaries.

If the cohort has no variation in `R`, no meaningful evidence across the assessment, extensive bounds/caps, or conspicuous conflicts between processed marks and the descriptors, flag the outputs for moderation before release. Do not invent differences or silently adjust selected students. The target is a moderation choice, not evidence that a weak cohort has met a standard. If a descriptor conflict persists after evidenced scoring corrections, record that the target and criterion-referenced judgement cannot both be accepted unchanged; retain the provisional result and resolve the policy choice with the tutor.

If a target SD is later explicitly authorised, use the previous starting slope `b = S / population_SD(R)` and replace 100 by `b` in the conversion. Recheck the feasible offset bracket. Bounds and eligibility caps generally prevent an exact final SD of `S`; report the achieved SD and do not promise both moments without a separately specified constrained solution. A positive SD cannot be generated from identical raw overalls without inventing differences.

## 8. Whole-number marks, ties and weighted-grade checks

Keep full precision throughout scoring averages, weighting, offset solving and penalties. Display criterion percentages with nearest-integer rounding, halves upwards. Calculate the overall from **unrounded** criteria, never the displayed integers.

For the cohort's academic overall integers `H_i`, use mean-preserving rounding of the post-cap values `G_i`:

1. Set `F_i = floor(G_i)` and `r_i = G_i − F_i`. Treat values within `1e-8` of an integer as that integer to prevent numerical noise.
2. The desired total is `L = N × 68`. For the current 17-student inventory, `L = 1,156`.
3. Set `K = L − sum(F_i)`. Ordinarily, round up the `K` largest remainders and leave the others rounded down. Never exceed a student's eligibility ceiling.
4. Preserve identical post-cap academic marks as ties: students with the same `G` must receive the same academic integer. If the ordinary allocation would split a tie, select the floor/ceiling allocation for whole tied groups that achieves `L` with the smallest total squared rounding error. This is a small finite allocation problem for this cohort, not a reason to split identical marks by participant ID.
5. If no tie-preserving floor/ceiling allocation achieves `L`, prioritise equal treatment of ties. Choose the attainable total nearest `L`, then minimise total squared rounding error. Report the resulting small deviation from 68 explicitly. For exact remaining allocation ties, prefer the lower total, then a deterministic ordering of the groups by unrounded mark.
6. Report the actual mean and population SD after rounding. Exact integer mean, tie preservation and a prescribed SD cannot always all be satisfied.

Each rounded overall must be the floor or ceiling of its unrounded post-cap value. Do not shift individual marks by several points under the label of rounding. The student's recorded chain must show:

`unrounded weighted overall U → eligibility-capped G → cohort-rounded academic H → word-count deduction → final mark`

The displayed integer criterion marks may not reproduce `H` exactly when reweighted. This is a rounding consequence, and an explicit cap can create a larger difference. Show unrounded contributions and the cap/rounding steps in the internal audit rather than claiming that displayed components sum exactly.

## 9. Word count and final mark

The limit is **1,500 words**. The supplied policy applies a **10 percentage-point deduction when the assessed count exceeds 1,650 words**. Exactly 1,650 does not trigger it. This is subtraction of 10 points, not multiplication by 0.90.

Record the declared count, independently assessed count, counting basis and any exclusions. The supplied extracts do not establish the full institutional rules for references, tables or appendices; verify those rules before applying a borderline penalty rather than inventing exclusions. If a count remains disputed, record the penalty as pending.

`D_i = 10 if confirmed assessed count > 1650, otherwise 0`

`Final_i = max(0, H_i − D_i)`

There is **no automatic deduction for underlength**. Score actual omissions or thin analysis under the relevant rows. Do not count excess length twice by adding an automatic presentation deduction.

The target mean of 68 applies before this penalty. Do not renormalise afterwards, as that would partly cancel the published deduction. Report both academic and final cohort means. If all affected marks are at least 10 and the academic mean is exactly 68, the final mean is `68 − 10 × (number penalised / N)`; otherwise use the actual final values, including the zero floor and any documented rounding deviation.
