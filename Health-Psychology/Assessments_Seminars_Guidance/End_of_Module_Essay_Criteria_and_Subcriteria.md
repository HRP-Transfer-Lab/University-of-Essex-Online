# End-of-Module Essay: Criteria and Diagnostic Subcriteria

## Assessment

**Unit 9 End-of-Module Essay**

**Word count:** 2,200 words.

The student selects one chronic illness and addresses all four required areas:

1. impact of the illness on the individual, including prevalence, adjustment and quality of life;
2. self-management challenges and what theoretical models tell us about coping with chronic illness;
3. critical evaluation of current behaviour-change interventions;
4. proposal of an intervention to promote optimal wellbeing, based on theory, research and the preceding analysis.

Source documents include:

- `L5 Assignment Grading Criteria (3).pdf`
- `syllabus and assessments.md`
- module reading list and seminar/formative materials

## Official criteria and weightings

1. **Knowledge and Understanding of the Subject Area / Conceptual Issues — 25%**
2. **Application of Theory to Practice and/or Real-world Example — 25%**
3. **Critical Analysis and Evaluation — 25%**
4. **Reading and Referencing — 15%**
5. **Presentation Style and Structure — 10%**

The four diagnostic subcriteria below sit underneath each official criterion.

---

## 1. Knowledge and Understanding — 25%

### 1.1 Knowledge of the selected chronic illness and health burden
The student demonstrates accurate and relevant understanding of the selected condition, including prevalence, consequences and appropriate health-related context.

### 1.2 Understanding of adjustment and quality of life
The student demonstrates knowledge of psychological adjustment, functioning, wellbeing and quality-of-life issues associated with the condition. Strong work should connect illness-specific evidence with seminar-led scholarship on lived experience and identity, particularly **Charmaz (1983)** where relevant.

### 1.3 Understanding of self-management challenges
The student identifies and accurately explains important self-management demands such as adherence, symptom management, coping, behavioural regulation or treatment burden.

### 1.4 Understanding of relevant psychological theory and biopsychosocial influences
The student accurately explains relevant health-psychology theories/models and demonstrates understanding of interacting biological, psychological and social influences where appropriate. Where illness representations or self-regulation are used, **Hagger & Orbell (2003)** should provide an important evidence base rather than theory being presented only from generic summaries.

---

## 2. Application of Theory to Practice / Real-world Example — 25%

### 2.1 Theory applied to illness experience and adjustment
Relevant psychological theory is explicitly used to explain how individuals perceive, experience or adjust to the selected chronic illness. Seminar-led readings should be applied where relevant, for example **Charmaz (1983)** for identity/lived experience and **Hagger & Orbell (2003)** for illness representations and coping.

### 2.2 Theory applied to self-management
The student applies theoretical constructs to specific self-management behaviours or challenges, such as adherence, coping, symptom interpretation or lifestyle change.

### 2.3 Theory applied to existing interventions
The student identifies how current interventions target theoretically relevant mechanisms, determinants or behaviour-change processes.

### 2.4 Theory-driven proposed intervention
The proposed intervention follows logically from the identified theoretical mechanisms, empirical evidence and needs of the selected population, with clear links between intervention components and intended outcomes. Strong proposals should show awareness of **intervention level and context**, drawing where relevant on Seminar 5 material such as **Grant & Kinman (2014)** and/or **West et al. (2020)** to distinguish individual-level support from organisational/system-level change.

---

## 3. Critical Analysis and Evaluation — 25%

### 3.1 Critical evaluation of psychological theory
The student evaluates strengths, limitations, assumptions and explanatory adequacy of the relevant theories rather than relying on description alone.

### 3.2 Critical evaluation of empirical evidence
The student compares and evaluates research evidence for methodological quality, consistency, limitations, generalisability and implications. Seminar-led readings should be interrogated rather than merely reported: for example, **Hagger & Orbell (2003)** raises questions about what correlational meta-analytic evidence can establish about causal pathways, and **Broadbent et al. (2004)** allows critique of prediction, measurement and clinical interpretation.

### 3.3 Critical evaluation of existing interventions
The student evaluates the effectiveness, mechanisms, limitations and applicability of current behaviour-change or psychological interventions for the selected condition.

### 3.4 Synthesis, judgement and justification of the proposed intervention
The student synthesises theory and evidence into coherent judgements and uses that synthesis to justify the proposed intervention, including appropriate acknowledgement of uncertainty, limitations or alternative approaches.

---

## 4. Reading and Referencing — 15%

### 4.1 Substantive engagement with seminar-led/module reading and wider scholarship
The student demonstrates substantive engagement with relevant seminar-led/module scholarship and meaningful illness-specific wider reading. Strong work should normally draw on **Charmaz (1983)** for adjustment/lived experience where relevant, **Hagger & Orbell (2003)** for illness representations/self-regulation where relevant, and **Broadbent et al. (2004)** when discussing illness beliefs, measurement or intervention targets. **Grant & Kinman (2014)** and/or **West et al. (2020)** are relevant where intervention level, professional support or implementation context is discussed.

### 4.2 Quality, relevance and currency of sources
The literature base makes appropriate use of peer-reviewed primary research, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, authoritative sources and relevant contemporary evidence alongside seminal theory where appropriate.

### 4.3 Evidence-to-claim alignment
Citations are used accurately and substantively, with sources genuinely supporting the claims, interpretations and intervention rationale for which they are cited.

### 4.4 APA citation and reference accuracy
In-text citations and the reference list are complete, consistent and appropriately presented in APA style.

---

## 5. Presentation Style and Structure — 10%

### 5.1 Coverage and organisation of the four assignment requirements
The essay clearly addresses all four required components and allocates appropriate attention to each.

### 5.2 Coherence and progression of argument
Sections and paragraphs are logically connected, with the discussion building towards the intervention proposal and final conclusions.

### 5.3 Academic writing and conceptual precision
Writing is clear, concise, appropriately formal and precise in its use of psychological terminology.

### 5.4 Technical presentation and adherence to requirements
Grammar, spelling, punctuation, paragraphing, headings, referencing presentation, anonymity requirements and the 2,200-word limit are handled appropriately.

---

# Diagnostic use

For each subcriterion:

- **1** = fully and convincingly satisfied
- **0.5** = partially, unevenly or ambiguously satisfied
- **0** = absent, seriously incorrect or not meaningfully demonstrated

Use these values diagnostically. They are not to be converted mechanically into the official criterion percentage. The official criterion mark should be selected holistically using the University's grade-band descriptors.


---

# Reading-engagement anchors

The seminar-led sources are **anchors, not a closed bibliography**. Students should also use current literature specific to their selected chronic illness.

Substantive engagement includes accurately explaining, applying, comparing or critiquing a source's concepts, findings, methods or limitations.

The following do **not** count as substantive engagement:

- a source appearing only in the reference list;
- a token citation attached to a generic statement;
- repeating an abstract or seminar slide without application;
- citing a source that does not support the claim;
- naming a theory without engaging with its constructs or evidence.

For a **2:1 or above**, there should normally be identifiable engagement with relevant seminar/module scholarship alongside illness-specific research. For a **First**, seminar-led scholarship should be synthesised with wider contemporary evidence and used in the student's reasoning rather than treated as background citation.
