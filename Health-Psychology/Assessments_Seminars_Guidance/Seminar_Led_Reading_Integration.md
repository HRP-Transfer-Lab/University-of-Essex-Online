# Seminar-Led Reading Integration

## Purpose

This guidance links each Health Psychology seminar to a small number of named readings that students are expected to **use**, not merely cite. The aim is to make module scholarship visible in seminar discussion and then traceable into assessment work.

The design principle is:

**read → use in seminar → evaluate/apply in assessment**

A citation in a reference list does not by itself demonstrate engagement. Students should use a reading's concepts, findings, methods, limitations or practical implications in their reasoning.

The existing seminar slides remain the source for the seminar topics. The adaptations below add one **core reading** and, where useful, one **supplementary reading** that can animate a concrete seminar activity.

---

# Seminar 1 — Introduction to Health Psychology

## Existing seminar focus

The seminar introduces definitions of health, the biomedical and biopsychosocial models, health behaviours and social-cognition approaches. It explicitly asks students to consider what it means to be healthy and how useful health-behaviour models are.

## Core reading

### Borrell-Carrió, F., Suchman, A. L., & Epstein, R. M. (2004). *The biopsychosocial model 25 years later: Principles, practice, and scientific inquiry.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1370/afm.245
- Full text: https://www.annfammed.org/content/2/6/576

### Why this reading

The paper treats the biopsychosocial model as both a philosophy of clinical care and a practical guide. It emphasises that health and illness operate across multiple levels, from social context to molecular processes, and that patients' subjective experience is a legitimate part of understanding outcomes. It also adds useful critical qualifications: mind–body relationships are complex rather than reducible to physiology; causal relations can be reciprocal; and participatory clinical relationships are culturally situated.

This moves the seminar beyond a static three-circle definition of the biopsychosocial model and gives students material they can evaluate.

## Supplementary reading

### Lehman, B. J., David, D. M., & Gruber, J. A. (2017). *Rethinking the biopsychosocial model of health: Understanding health as a dynamic system.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12328

### Why useful

Lehman et al. extend the biopsychosocial model using dynamic-systems thinking. Biological, psychological, interpersonal and wider contextual influences are treated as reciprocal and changing over time. This provides a useful contrast with a simple additive model.

## Suggested seminar adaptation

**Activity: From Venn diagram to dynamic explanation**

Give students a health behaviour such as exercise, alcohol consumption or sugar intake.

1. Map biological, psychological and social influences.
2. Identify at least two reciprocal relationships between them.
3. Ask which influences are likely to change across time or context.
4. Compare the resulting explanation with a purely individual social-cognition model.
5. Conclude: what does a biopsychosocial account add, and what does it fail to predict precisely?

## Assessment connection

This reading primarily supports CLQ subcriteria **C1.2, C1.4 and C2.1**, particularly where students recognise contextual limits of individual-level behaviour models.

# Seminar 2 — Health Promotion

## Existing seminar focus

The seminar addresses health-promotion campaigns, message framing, Theory of Planned Behaviour, individual differences, health inequalities and preparation for the CLQ poster. It already uses face-covering behaviour as an applied example and asks students to critique the limits of the TPB.

## Core reading

### Barile, J. P., Guerin, R. J., Fisher, K. A., et al. (2021). *Theory-based behavioral predictors of self-reported use of face coverings in public settings during the COVID-19 pandemic in the United States.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/abm/kaaa109
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7799273/

### Why this reading

Barile et al. provide a concrete empirical example of using theory-derived constructs to predict intention and reported health behaviour. In a large cross-sectional US sample, perceived social expectations, confidence to wear a face covering, and perceived importance of personal mask use were associated with intention. The design also creates useful critical questions: predictors were measured rather than manipulated; behaviour was self-reported; and cross-sectional associations do not establish causal mechanisms.

The paper therefore supports both **application** of theory and **critical evaluation** of evidence.

## Supplementary reading

### Ilic, D., & Rowe, N. (2013). *What is the evidence that poster presentations are effective in promoting knowledge transfer? A state of the art review.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/hir.12015
- Full text: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hir.12015

### Why useful

The review found that the evidence base for posters is much weaker than their widespread use might suggest. Posters can contribute to changes in knowledge, attitudes and behaviour, particularly as part of broader educational activity, while visual design can influence knowledge transfer. However, the included evidence was methodologically limited and did not directly compare posters with alternative educational approaches.

This gives students an evidence-based way to discuss why poster design matters without treating attractive presentation as sufficient evidence of behaviour change.

## Suggested seminar adaptation

**Activity: Build and critique a theory-led message**

1. Extract the main predictors in Barile et al.
2. Map them onto relevant TPB/self-efficacy constructs.
3. Identify which predictor could be turned into a poster message.
4. Identify one causal claim the study cannot justify.
5. Use Ilic & Rowe to propose two design principles for communicating the message.
6. Rework the same message using HBM or self-efficacy and compare what changes.

## Theory-specific module reading for the CLQ

Students should also engage with a source that directly defines/evaluates the model they select:

- **Health Belief Model:** Janz & Becker (1984) and/or Champion & Skinner (2008)
- **Theory of Planned Behaviour:** Montaño & Kasprzyk (2008); Barile et al. (2021) provides applied evidence
- **Self-efficacy:** Bandura (1977) and/or Bandura (1998/2004)

## Assessment connection

This seminar reading pathway directly supports CLQ subcriteria **C1.1–C1.4, C2.1–C2.3, C3.1–C3.2, C4.1–C4.3 and C5.3**.

# Seminar 3 — Stress, Health, Coping and Adapting to Chronic Illness

## Existing seminar focus

The seminar examines stress appraisal, stress-health pathways, coping, chronic-illness diagnosis, adjustment, family impact and the role of the health psychologist. It explicitly asks students to consider barriers to successful adaptation to chronic illness.

## Core reading

### Charmaz, K. (1983). *Loss of self: A fundamental form of suffering in the chronically ill.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9566.ep10491512
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10261981/

### Why this reading

Charmaz's qualitative study of 57 chronically ill adults argues that suffering cannot be reduced to physical discomfort. Chronic illness can undermine valued identities through restricted lives, social isolation, being discredited and feeling that one burdens others. This provides a strong counterweight to a purely biomedical account of disease impact and makes adjustment, self-concept and social context concrete.

It is especially useful for the final essay's first question on adjustment and quality of life.

## Supplementary reading

### Keller, A., Litzelman, K., Wisk, L. E., et al. (2012). *Does the perception that stress affects health matter? The association with health and mortality.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026743
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3374921/

### Why useful

Keller et al. used a large US population dataset linked to mortality records. High reported stress combined with a strong perception that stress affected health was associated with increased mortality risk. The study is useful precisely because it invites methodological discussion: it is observational, perception and stress are self-reported, and association should not be converted into a simple causal claim that “believing stress is harmful causes death”.

## Suggested seminar adaptation

**Activity: What does “impact of chronic illness” actually mean?**

1. Use Charmaz to identify four forms of identity/social disruption.
2. Apply them to a chronic-illness case.
3. Contrast these with prevalence, symptoms and biomedical burden.
4. Use Keller et al. to distinguish association, prediction and causation.
5. Ask what evidence would be needed before designing an intervention around stress perceptions.

## Assessment connection

This seminar reading pathway directly supports final-essay subcriteria **C1.1–C1.3, C2.1, C3.2 and C4.1–C4.3**.

# Seminar 4 — Illness and Treatment Beliefs

## Existing seminar focus

The seminar centres on Leventhal's self-regulation/Common-Sense Model, illness representations, coping and appraisal. It presents evidence from Hagger & Orbell and uses the Broadbent myocardial-infarction heart-drawing study as a concrete example.

## Core reading

### Hagger, M. S., & Orbell, S. (2003). *A meta-analytic review of the Common-Sense Model of illness representations.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/088704403100081321
- Essex repository: https://repository.essex.ac.uk/12676/

### Why this reading

The meta-analysis synthesised 45 studies using the Common-Sense Model. It found coherent relationships among illness-representation dimensions such as consequences, control/cure, identity and timeline, and linked illness cognitions to coping and outcomes across illness types. It also gives students an opportunity to ask what a correlational synthesis can and cannot establish about causal pathways from representations through coping to outcomes.

This is a strong bridge from theoretical description to evidence-based evaluation.

## Supplementary reading

### Broadbent, E., Petrie, K. J., Ellis, C. J., Ying, J., & Gamble, G. (2004). *A picture of health—myocardial infarction patients' drawings of their hearts and subsequent disability.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2004.03.014
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15596165/

### Why useful

Patients' drawings of heart damage after myocardial infarction predicted later perceptions of recovery and speed of return to work. The study turns an abstract concept—illness representation—into observable patient-generated material and invites discussion of prediction, measurement, possible confounding and clinical use.

## Suggested seminar adaptation

**Activity: From illness representation to intervention target**

1. Extract the Common-Sense Model dimensions from Hagger & Orbell.
2. Identify which dimensions show the clearest relationships with coping/outcomes.
3. Examine the Broadbent heart drawings and outcomes.
4. Decide which illness belief(s) an intervention might target.
5. State the hypothesised mechanism: **belief → coping/self-management → outcome**.
6. Identify one limitation in the evidence that makes the mechanism uncertain.

## Assessment connection

This seminar reading pathway directly supports final-essay subcriteria **C1.3–C1.4, C2.1–C2.4, C3.1–C3.4 and C4.1–C4.3**.

# Seminar 5 — The Role of Health Psychology in Supporting Healthcare Professionals

## Existing seminar focus

The seminar considers communication, compassion fatigue, resilience, psychologically safe workplaces, behaviour-change techniques, intervention development and outcome evaluation. It contrasts individual strategies with organisational conditions.

## Core reading

### Grant, L., & Kinman, G. (2014). *Emotional resilience in the helping professions and how it can be enhanced.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.11120/hsce.2014.00040

### Why this reading

Grant & Kinman review emotional resilience in helping professions and identify competencies including emotional literacy, reflective ability, appropriate empathy and social competence. They discuss strategies such as reflective practice, supervision, peer coaching, mindfulness and experiential learning. Importantly, the paper does not reduce resilience to an individual responsibility: it also stresses that adequate resources, job control, support and role clarity are organisational responsibilities.

This creates a useful intervention-design question: when should an intervention target the individual, and when should it change the environment?

## Supplementary reading

### West, M., Bailey, S., & Williams, E. (2020). *The courage of compassion: Supporting nurses and midwives to deliver high-quality care.*

- The King's Fund: https://www.kingsfund.org.uk/insight-and-analysis/reports/courage-compassion-supporting-nurses-midwives

### Why useful

The report identifies autonomy, belonging and contribution as core work needs and proposes system-level recommendations concerning workload, teamworking, leadership, supervision, work conditions and development. It explicitly argues for changing causes of workplace stress rather than only treating symptoms.

Together, Grant & Kinman and West et al. support a critical comparison of individual-level and organisational intervention strategies.

## Suggested seminar adaptation

**Activity: Individual intervention or system intervention?**

1. Identify the intervention strategies in Grant & Kinman.
2. Categorise each as individual, interpersonal/team or organisational.
3. Compare with West et al.'s autonomy–belonging–contribution framework.
4. Decide which level should be prioritised for compassion fatigue.
5. Specify an outcome measure and an evaluation design.
6. Transfer the same reasoning to a chronic-illness intervention: does the proposed intervention place too much responsibility on the patient?

## Assessment connection

This seminar reading pathway primarily supports final-essay subcriteria **C2.3–C2.4, C3.3–C3.4 and C4.1–C4.3**, especially when judging intervention mechanisms, feasibility, context and unintended individual-blame.

# Assessment reading-engagement principle

The assessments should reward **substantive use** of seminar-led readings rather than citation presence.

Substantive engagement may include:

- accurately explaining a reading's theoretical construct or finding;
- applying it to the chosen behaviour or illness;
- comparing it with another source or model;
- evaluating its method, assumptions or inferential limits;
- using it to justify or challenge an intervention component;
- transferring an insight from the seminar example to the student's chosen topic.

The following do **not** count as substantive engagement:

- a source appearing only in the reference list;
- a token parenthetical citation attached to a generic statement;
- repeating the abstract or seminar slide without application;
- citing a source whose content does not support the claim;
- naming a theory without engaging with its constructs or evidence.

## CLQ expected reading pathway

For strong work, students should normally demonstrate:

1. substantive engagement with **Barile et al. (2021)** as the Seminar 2 core applied reading;
2. substantive engagement with **at least one model-specific module source** relevant to their chosen HBM, TPB or self-efficacy framework;
3. appropriate wider empirical reading concerning the selected behaviour;
4. where poster communication/design is discussed, **Ilic & Rowe (2013)** provides a particularly relevant seminar-linked source.

Borrell-Carrió et al. (2004), and optionally Lehman et al. (2017), can strengthen discussion of contextual or biopsychosocial limits of individual-level models.

## Final essay expected reading pathway

For strong work, students should normally demonstrate:

1. substantive engagement with **Charmaz (1983)** when analysing adjustment, lived experience, identity or quality of life;
2. substantive engagement with **Hagger & Orbell (2003)** when using/evaluating illness representations or the Common-Sense Model;
3. use of **Broadbent et al. (2004)** where illness beliefs, measurement or intervention targets are relevant;
4. use of **Grant & Kinman (2014)** and/or **West et al. (2020)** where intervention level, professional support, implementation context or individual-versus-system responsibility is relevant;
5. substantial **illness-specific wider reading** beyond the module sources.

The seminar-led sources are anchors, not a closed bibliography. Strong assessment work should integrate them with current literature specific to the student's chosen behaviour or chronic illness.
