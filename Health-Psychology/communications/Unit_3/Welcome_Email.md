## Unit 3: Theory-led health promotion

As you work through the unit, focus particularly on the connection between:

**theory → evidence → health message**

A strong health-promotion message should not simply mention a psychological theory. It should be possible to identify the specific theoretical construct being targeted, explain why that construct should influence behaviour, and support that reasoning with appropriate research evidence.

This is also a useful point to keep asking yourself:

- Which psychological construct am I trying to change?
- Why should changing that construct affect the target behaviour?
- What evidence supports that link?
- Does my message actually reflect the theory I have chosen?
- Am I making claims that the evidence can genuinely support?

These questions are central to both this week's learning and your first assessment.

## Your CLQ begins this week

The **Unit 3–4 Collaborative Learning Question (CLQ)** starts now.

For your initial post, you need to create a **theory-led health-promotion poster** and accompany it with a **500-word executive summary**.

You should choose **one** of the following health behaviours:

- discouraging excessive alcohol consumption;
- promoting 30 minutes of exercise per day;
- reducing daily sugar intake.

You should then apply **one** of the following approaches:

- the Health Belief Model;
- the Theory of Planned Behaviour;
- self-efficacy theory.

Your poster should be clear, engaging and easy to understand, with a focused health message rather than large amounts of text. The executive summary is where you should explain **how the theory informed the poster** and **how research evidence informed the health-promotion message**.

Importantly, your **initial post must be submitted during Unit 3**. In Unit 4, you will then critically respond to **two peers' posters**, with approximately **200 words per response**. All three contributions form part of the assessment.

Try not to approach the task as “describe a theory and then design a poster”. Instead, work from the theory itself: identify a construct, decide how it could be targeted, and then design a message that follows logically from that reasoning.

## Seminar 2: Health Promotion

This week's seminar is designed to help you do exactly that.

Our **core reading** is:

**Barile, J. P., Guerin, R. J., Fisher, K. A., et al. (2021). _Theory-based behavioral predictors of self-reported use of face coverings in public settings during the COVID-19 pandemic in the United States._ Annals of Behavioral Medicine.**

Full text: [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7799273/](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7799273/)

Barile et al. give us a useful example of how theory-derived constructs can be linked to intentions and reported health behaviour. Just as importantly, the study allows us to think critically about what cross-sectional, self-report evidence can — and cannot — tell us about behaviour change.

The **supplementary reading** is:

**Ilic, D., & Rowe, N. (2013). _What is the evidence that poster presentations are effective in promoting knowledge transfer? A state of the art review._**

Full text: [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hir.12015](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hir.12015)

This gives us a useful evidence-based way to think about poster communication and design, while also reminding us that an attractive poster is not, by itself, evidence that behaviour will change.

In the seminar, we will use these readings to:

- identify theory-based predictors of behaviour;
- map them onto relevant theoretical constructs;
- turn a construct into a possible health-promotion message;
- distinguish association from causal evidence;
- consider how poster design affects communication;
- compare how the same health message might change when approached using a different theory.

If you can, come to the seminar with a **rough one-sentence health message** for one of the three CLQ behaviours. We can use these as examples when thinking about whether a message is genuinely theory-led.

## Choosing and supporting your theory

Whichever theory you choose for the CLQ, make sure you engage with a source that directly explains and evaluates that model.

Useful module-linked starting points include:

- **Health Belief Model:** Janz & Becker (1984) and/or Champion & Skinner (2008);
- **Theory of Planned Behaviour:** Montaño & Kasprzyk (2008), alongside Barile et al. (2021) as applied evidence;
- **Self-efficacy:** Bandura (1977) and/or Bandura's later work on self-efficacy and health behaviour.

The strongest work will combine this theory-specific reading with research directly relevant to your chosen behaviour.

The main goal this week is therefore not simply to produce a visually appealing poster. It is to make the logic connecting **theory, research evidence and the final health message** clear and convincing.
