Dear everyone,

Welcome to **Biological Psychology**. I’m Mark, and I’ll be your tutor for the module.

Over the next nine units, we’ll look at how thoughts, emotions and behaviour can be understood in relation to the nervous system and the brain. We’ll begin with the foundations of the field, then move through the nervous system and research methods before applying these ideas to perception and attention, consciousness, learning and memory, language, emotion, and psychiatric disorders.

A recurring theme throughout the module will be that biological evidence needs to be interpreted carefully. It isn’t enough to identify a brain region or report that an area was active. We’ll keep asking questions such as: *What was actually measured? What can this method tell us? Does the evidence show an association, a necessary brain system, or a causal relationship? What might the study not allow us to conclude?*

Those questions will be especially important for your first assessment.

## Unit 1: Introduction to Biological Psychology

Unit 1 gives us the conceptual foundations for the rest of the module. In particular, we’ll consider:

- what Biological Psychology is and how it relates to psychology, neuroscience and biology;
- different **levels of analysis**, from molecular and cellular processes through to brain systems and behaviour;
- how ideas about brain–behaviour relationships have developed historically;
- the move from early localisation ideas to modern empirical neuroscience;
- some of the main methods used to study relationships between the brain and behaviour;
- the interaction between genetic and environmental influences, including epigenetics.

For your Unit 1 reading, the main starting points are:

- **Lyons et al. (2014), _Biological Psychology_, Chapters 2 and 3** — *The Brain* and *The Nervous System*;
- **Ward (2015), _The Student’s Guide to Cognitive Neuroscience_, Chapter 1** — for historical and conceptual context.

The module also lists the NeuroBriefs video **_The Ascent: A Brief History of the Brain_** as required material.

You don’t need to memorise everything. Focus on building a clear sense of how Biological Psychology asks questions about the relationship between biological processes and psychological experience.

## Seminar 1

Our first seminar is also in **Unit 1** and will introduce the main ideas that we’ll keep returning to across the module.

We’ll look at:

- what distinguishes Biological Psychology from other approaches;
- different levels of analysis;
- historical ideas about localisation and brain function;
- research methods such as neuroimaging, EEG, psychophysiology, behavioural approaches and lesion studies;
- strengths and limitations of different methods;
- gene–environment interaction and epigenetics.

There will be plenty of opportunity to discuss the material, so please come ready to contribute even if it’s only a short comment or question.

A useful question to think about before the seminar is:

> **If a brain-imaging study shows that a particular area becomes active during a task, what can we reasonably conclude — and what would be an overclaim?**

That distinction is central to Biological Psychology and will become important very quickly in the first assessment.

### Core seminar reading

For the seminar, I’d particularly like you to look at:

**Poldrack, R. A., & Wagner, A. D. (2004). _What can neuroimaging tell us about the mind? Insights from prefrontal cortex._ Current Directions in Psychological Science, 13(5), 177–181.**

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2004.00302.x

You don’t need to understand every methodological detail. As you read it, focus on this question:

> **Why doesn’t activity in a brain region automatically tell us which mental process is taking place?**

We’ll use this in the seminar to think about the difference between an **observation**, a **reasonable inference**, and an **overclaim**.

If you want to go a little further, the supplementary reading is:

**Henson, R. (2005). _What can functional neuroimaging tell the experimental psychologist?_ The Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology Section A, 58(2), 193–233.**

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/02724980443000502

## Looking ahead to the first assessment

Your first summative assessment is the **Collaborative Learning Question (CLQ)**, which starts in **Unit 2** and finishes at the end of **Unit 3**.

For the Initial Response, you’ll identify one **empirical human study** investigating a relationship between the structure or function of the nervous system and human thought, emotion and/or behaviour. You’ll briefly explain the study’s aim, procedure and findings, and then make clear what brain–behaviour relationship the study demonstrates.

You’ll then write **two peer responses** in Unit 3, evaluating the strengths and limitations of the methods used in your peers’ chosen studies.

The assessed contributions are:

- **Initial Response — 500 words**
- **Peer Response 1 — 300 words**
- **Peer Response 2 — 300 words**

All three assessed responses are required.

We’ll build towards this gradually. Unit 1 is about giving you the conceptual tools to recognise a good brain–behaviour question and, importantly, to think carefully about what different kinds of evidence allow us to conclude.

As we move into Units 2 and 3, we’ll add the nervous-system detail and the research-method knowledge you’ll need to evaluate studies confidently.

Please work through the Unit 1 materials before the seminar if you can, and bring along any questions. You don’t need to arrive with everything mastered — the seminar is there to help us work through the ideas together.

I’m looking forward to working with you over the module.
