# Seminar-Led Reading Integration — Biological Psychology

## Purpose

The seminar-reading design follows the principle:

**read → use in seminar → apply/evaluate in assessment**

The seminars are delivered in **Units 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9**, regardless of their topical labels. Reading choices therefore reflect both the seminar content and what students need to do at that point in the assessment cycle.

A source only counts as meaningful assessment engagement when the student uses its concepts, findings, methods, inferential limits or implications. A token citation or bibliography entry does not count.

---

# Seminar 1 — Unit 1
## Introduction to Biological Psychology

### Assessment role

Early preparation for the Units 2–3 CLQ.

The seminar introduces levels of analysis, historical localisation, neuroimaging, EEG, psychophysiology, lesion approaches, gene–environment interaction and epigenetics.

## Core reading

### Poldrack, R. A., & Wagner, A. D. (2004). *What can neuroimaging tell us about the mind? Insights from prefrontal cortex.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2004.00302.x

### Why this reading

The paper introduces a central inferential problem in cognitive neuroscience: **reverse inference**. Brain activation can help constrain psychological theories, but activation in a region does not automatically prove that a specific cognitive process occurred.

This reading is short enough for early-module use and prepares students to distinguish:

- what a brain-imaging study measures;
- what relationship it demonstrates;
- what causal claim it does **not** necessarily justify.

These distinctions are directly relevant to the CLQ, where students must select an empirical study linking nervous-system structure/function to thought, emotion or behaviour.

## Supplementary reading

### Henson, R. (2005). *What can functional neuroimaging tell the experimental psychologist?*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/02724980443000502
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15903115/

### Why useful

Henson develops the logic of function–structure mapping more systematically and treats imaging measures as another dependent variable that can test competing psychological theories. It provides a deeper extension for students ready to think carefully about what imaging evidence contributes beyond behavioural data.

## Suggested seminar use

**Prompt:** “A study reports increased activity in region X during task Y. What can we conclude?”

Students add short comments under three headings:

1. **Observed**
2. **Reasonable inference**
3. **Overclaim**

Then ask: *Would a lesion study answer the same question in the same way?*

## Assessment connection

Supports CLQ:

- Knowledge & Understanding: C1.2–C1.4
- Critical Analysis: C2.1–C2.3
- Reading & Referencing: C4.1–C4.3

# Seminar 2 — Unit 3
## Structure and Function of the Nervous System

### Assessment role

Delivered while students are completing the CLQ and, especially, the two peer responses that require evaluation of biological-psychology methods.

## Core reading

### Fellows, L. K., Heberlein, A. S., Morales, D. A., Shivde, G., Waller, S., & Wu, D. H. (2005). *Method matters: An empirical study of impact in cognitive neuroscience.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1162/0898929054021139
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15969904/

### Why this reading

The paper directly contrasts **lesion studies** and **functional neuroimaging**. A key distinction is inferential:

- lesion evidence can support claims about whether a structure is **necessary** for a function;
- functional imaging is necessarily correlational and shows association rather than necessity.

The paper also found that functional-imaging articles were cited more often than lesion studies, illustrating that scientific visibility and inferential strength are not the same thing.

This makes it particularly useful for CLQ peer responses, because students need to discuss strengths and weaknesses of the methods used in classmates' empirical studies.

## Supplementary reading

### Chatterjee, A. (2005). *A madness to the methods in cognitive neuroscience?*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1162/0898929054021085
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15969903/

### Why useful

Chatterjee comments directly on Fellows et al. and emphasises the complementary strengths of lesion and imaging approaches. The commentary also raises questions about why technically novel methods may dominate a field even when older methods provide stronger evidence for some causal questions.

## Suggested seminar use

**Prompt:** “Which method gives the stronger answer?”

Present two minimal study descriptions:

- fMRI activity in hippocampus during memory task;
- hippocampal lesion followed by selective memory impairment.

Students comment:

- What can each study show?
- What can it not show?
- Which method would you prefer for a claim about **necessity**?
- Which method is better for mapping a distributed network?

This can be used almost verbatim as rehearsal for the CLQ peer responses.

## Assessment connection

Supports CLQ:

- Knowledge & Understanding: C1.3–C1.4
- Critical Analysis: C2.1–C2.4
- Communication: C3.2–C3.4
- Reading & Referencing: C4.1–C4.3

# Seminar 3 — Unit 5
## Learning and Memory

### Assessment role

This seminar marks the transition from the CLQ to the final **narrative literature review**. The seminar itself already introduces what a narrative review is and its assessment structure.

## Core reading

### Snyder, H. (2019). *Literature review as a research methodology: An overview and guidelines.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2019.07.039

### Why this reading

Snyder treats literature review as a research methodology rather than a long descriptive essay. The paper discusses different forms of review, the importance of transparent search and selection procedures, synthesis, and common problems in weak reviews.

The key assessment lesson is that a narrative review should not simply summarise one paper after another.

## Supplementary reading

### Sukhera, J. (2022). *Narrative reviews: Flexible, rigorous, and practical.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.4300/JGME-D-22-00480.1
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9380636/

### Why useful

Sukhera argues that narrative reviews can be rigorous while retaining interpretive flexibility. This provides a useful framework for understanding how the final assessment can synthesise heterogeneous studies without becoming a systematic review.

## Topic-linked companion material

The seminar's learning-and-memory content can continue to use the module's H.M./Molaison and Eichenbaum material. These are useful examples on which to practise methodological critique and thematic synthesis, even though Snyder and Sukhera provide the core **assessment-method** reading.

## Suggested seminar use

**Prompt:** “Summary or synthesis?”

Show two short paragraphs:

- one organised paper-by-paper;
- one organised around a claim/theme and comparing evidence.

Students identify which better resembles a narrative review and add one reason.

Then ask students to turn three memory-study findings into a **theme heading**, rather than three study summaries.

## Assessment connection

Supports final review:

- Knowledge & Understanding: C1.3–C1.4
- Application: C2.2–C2.4
- Critical Analysis: C3.2–C3.4
- Reading & Referencing: C4.1–C4.3
- Presentation/Structure: C5.1–C5.3

# Seminar 4 — Unit 7
## Language and Evolution

### Assessment role

Delivered alongside the Unit 7 formative literature-search task. The reading should therefore model both substantive biological psychology and how a strong review organises a large evidence base.

## Core reading

### Price, C. J. (2012). *A review and synthesis of the first 20 years of PET and fMRI studies of heard speech, spoken language and reading.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2012.04.062
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3398395/

### Why this reading

Price synthesises hundreds of PET/fMRI studies and distinguishes:

- functions that are relatively localised;
- functions emerging from distributed patterns of activity;
- the importance of interactions between regions.

It is therefore useful twice over:

1. as a substantive reading about language networks;
2. as a worked example of how a review can organise a very large literature into coherent themes.

## Supplementary reading

### Friederici, A. D. (2011). *The brain basis of language processing: From structure to function.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00006.2011
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22013214/

### Why useful

Friederici reviews structural and functional evidence for language processing across hemispheres and networks. It provides a strong conceptual counterpoint to overly simple “Broca = production / Wernicke = comprehension” localisation.

## Suggested seminar use

**Prompt:** “From search results to themes”

Using three language papers from a mock database search, students decide whether each belongs under:

- speech perception;
- production;
- syntax;
- semantic processing;
- distributed/network explanations.

Then compare their categories with how Price structures the evidence.

This directly rehearses the Unit 7 formative task and the final review's thematic synthesis.

## Assessment connection

Supports final review:

- Knowledge & Understanding: C1.1–C1.4
- Application: C2.1–C2.3
- Critical Analysis: C3.1–C3.4
- Reading & Referencing: C4.1–C4.4
- Presentation/Structure: C5.1–C5.2

# Seminar 5 — Unit 9
## Psychiatric Disorders, Stress and Well-Being

### Assessment role

Final-stage support for the narrative literature review. The seminar is especially useful for practising **competing explanations**, **levels of analysis**, and avoiding biological reductionism.

## Core reading

### Banner, N. F. (2013). *Mental disorders are not brain disorders.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/jep.12048
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3708113/

### Why this reading

Banner challenges the claim that mental disorders should simply be reclassified as brain disorders. The paper accepts that mental disorders have neural correlates but argues that disorders are identified at the level of the person.

This is an excellent final-seminar reading because it forces students to distinguish:

- neural correlate from full explanation;
- biological mechanism from psychiatric construct;
- evidence from reductionist interpretation.

## Supplementary reading

### Howes, O. D., & Kapur, S. (2009). *The dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia: Version III—The final common pathway.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/schbul/sbp006
- Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2669582/

### Why useful

Howes and Kapur show how a biological theory develops rather than remaining static. Their version III reframes dopamine dysregulation as a final common pathway influenced by multiple upstream risk factors.

Placed alongside Banner, the paper supports a much stronger question than “Is schizophrenia caused by dopamine?”:

> What explanatory role should dopamine evidence play within a multi-level account of schizophrenia?

## Optional topic-specific extension

### McEwen, B. S. (2007). *Physiology and neurobiology of stress and adaptation: Central role of the brain.*

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00041.2006

Useful when the seminar or final review focuses strongly on stress, allostasis or adaptation.

## Suggested seminar use

**Prompt:** “Mechanism, correlate or explanation?”

Students classify claims such as:

- elevated presynaptic dopamine synthesis is associated with schizophrenia;
- dopamine dysregulation contributes to psychotic symptoms;
- schizophrenia is a dopamine disorder;
- schizophrenia is a brain disorder.

They then identify which step requires additional evidence or conceptual argument.

## Assessment connection

Supports final review:

- Knowledge & Understanding: C1.1–C1.4
- Application: C2.1–C2.4
- Critical Analysis: C3.1–C3.4
- Reading & Referencing: C4.1–C4.3

# Assessment reading-engagement principle

## CLQ

Strong work should normally demonstrate substantive use of:

1. the student's chosen **empirical primary study**;
2. at least one relevant **module/seminar methods reading**, especially Poldrack & Wagner (2004), Henson (2005), Fellows et al. (2005), or Chatterjee (2005);
3. appropriate additional scholarly sources supporting methodological evaluation.

The point is not citation counting. The student should use the reading to reason about what the method measures and what conclusions are justified.

## Final narrative review

Strong work should normally demonstrate:

1. substantive use of **Snyder (2019)** and/or **Sukhera (2022)** to inform review methodology and synthesis;
2. substantive use of relevant **topic-specific module readings** for the chosen review topic;
3. independent peer-reviewed literature beyond the provided reading list;
4. explicit comparison of methods, theories and findings rather than serial study summaries.

For topic areas represented in Seminars 4–5, Price (2012), Friederici (2011), Banner (2013), and Howes & Kapur (2009) are particularly strong seminar-linked anchors.

## What does not count

- source listed only in the reference list;
- token citation attached to a generic claim;
- repeating a seminar slide or abstract;
- naming a theory without explaining or evaluating it;
- citing a method paper without using it to constrain interpretation.
