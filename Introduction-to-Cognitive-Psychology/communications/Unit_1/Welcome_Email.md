Dear everyone,

Welcome to **Introduction to Cognitive Psychology**. I’m Mark, and I’ll be your tutor for the module.

Over the next nine units, we’ll look at how the mind takes in, processes, stores and uses information. We’ll move from perception and attention through memory, emotion, language, reasoning and decision-making, while also looking at how cognitive psychology can be applied in real-world settings.

A key feature of this module is that we’ll approach cognition from three related perspectives:

- **Cognitive psychology** — how information is processed in the mind;
- **Cognitive neuroscience** — how cognitive processes relate to activity in the brain;
- **Cognitive neuropsychology** — what brain injury and neurological illness can tell us about the organisation of cognition.

You’ll also take part in cognitive tasks and experiments during the module, so we’ll regularly connect theory with the kinds of methods psychologists use to study cognition.

## Unit 1: Approaches to Cognition

Unit 1 gives us the foundations for the rest of the module. We’ll introduce some of the central concepts you’ll keep coming back to, including:

- **cognition** and information processing;
- **metacognition** — thinking about and monitoring our own thinking;
- **mental representations**;
- **top-down and bottom-up processing**;
- **schema and predictive processing**;
- **functional brain networks**;
- the relationship between cognitive psychology, cognitive neuroscience and cognitive neuropsychology;
- ethical principles in cognitive research.

By the end of the unit, the aim is for you to be able to see how these three approaches overlap but also what each contributes.

As you work through the Unit 1 material, a useful question to keep in mind is:

> **What kind of evidence would each approach use to explain the same cognitive process?**

For example, cognitive psychology might focus on behaviour and performance, cognitive neuroscience might examine patterns of brain activity, and cognitive neuropsychology might ask what can be learned when a particular cognitive ability is disrupted following brain injury.

The Unit 1 lecturecast focuses on **ethics in cognitive research**.

It covers the four core principles from the British Psychological Society:

- **Respect**
- **Competence**
- **Responsibility**
- **Integrity**

It also considers how these principles apply across cognitive psychology, cognitive neuroscience and neuropsychology, and looks at ethical issues around the use of generative AI in academic work and research.

## Seminar 1: Ethics in Cognitive Psychology

Our first seminar is in **Unit 1** and is a 50-minute session focused on applying ethical principles to cognitive psychology.

We’ll look at:

- how the four BPS principles apply to cognitive research;
- ethical issues that arise when working with different participant groups;
- deception in psychological research;
- the benefits and risks of generative AI;
- how AI can be used in ways that support learning without undermining academic standards or ethical practice.

There will be several short discussion activities rather than long group exercises, so please come ready to contribute a comment, example or question.

### Core seminar reading

The main source for the seminar is the:

**British Psychological Society. _Code of Ethics and Conduct_.**

As you read it, don’t worry about memorising the document. Instead, think about how the four principles might guide decisions in a real research study.

A useful question to prepare is:

> **Is it ever ethically justifiable to use deception in psychological research?**

We’ll also discuss:

> **Can generative AI tools be used in ways that are genuinely helpful and ethically responsible, or do the risks outweigh the benefits?**

If you want to go a little further, the Unit 1 reading list also includes:

**Snyder, H. R., Miyake, A., & Hankin, B. L. (2015). _Advancing understanding of executive function impairments and psychopathology: Bridging the gap between clinical and cognitive approaches._ Frontiers in Psychology, 6, 328.**

This is useful for seeing how cognitive concepts can be linked to broader psychological and clinical questions.

## Looking ahead

In **Unit 2**, you’ll move into visual perception and attention and complete the **Change Blindness Cognitive Task**.

That task becomes the basis for your first summative assessment, the **Collaborative Learning Question (CLQ)**, which runs across Units 3 and 4.

For the CLQ, you’ll need to:

- explain the change-blindness phenomenon using research;
- relate your explanation to your own experience of the task;
- identify real-world situations where limits of attention matter;
- respond critically to peers using evidence from at least two of:
  - Cognitive Psychology;
  - Cognitive Neuroscience;
  - Cognitive Neuropsychology.

We’ll build towards this gradually. For now, Unit 1 is about getting comfortable with the main approaches to cognition and understanding the ethical standards that should guide research and academic work throughout the module.
